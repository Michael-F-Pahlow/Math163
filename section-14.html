    Subsection 14.1 Plots of Multi-Variable Functions

    The graph of a multi-variable is a surface that changes "height" (\(z\)-variable) over the \(xy-\)plane. This is difficult (at least for me) to draw accurately by hand, so we turn to a digital tool to help us. You can use GeoGebra 3D, Desmos 3D or CalcPlot3D to do this. For each tool simply type right side of the function into the input bar for the calculator.     Subsection 14.2 Contour Maps

    Another type of plot that we can make is called a contour plot or topographical map. A level curve is a two-dimensional plot for a constant value of \(z\text{.}\) This creates a slice of the surface parallel to the \(xy-\)plane. Collecting \(c=f(x,y)\) graphs for various constants forms a two-dimensional plot where the level curves show the contours or topography of the three-dimensional surface. Here is an example plot generated by Wolfram Alpha (the best tool for contour maps) for the function \(f(x,y)=\cos(x)\cos(y)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Wolfram Alpha colors this map so that peaks are lighter in color and valleys are darker. Contour maps may also be labeled with the "elevation" or constant of each level curve like in this plot.
    🔗
    Here is a contour or topographic map of the Mauna Loa volcano in Hawaii. The contours or level curves are at increments at every 1000 feet as noted on the map. The scale of the \(xy\)-plane is given at the bottom in kilometers.
    🔗
    From this contour we can note a few things. Concentric circles that have larger values as you move to the center represent a peak or maximum. Using the vocabulary you learned in Calculus I (Math 151), there are three local maximum values on this map. The main peak (Mauna Loa) near the center of the map with an elevation of over 13,000 feet. Another local maximum and slightly shorter peak (Mauna Kea) is just above this on the map with an elevation of over 12,000 feet. A third unnamed peak or local maximum is in the top left of the map with an elevation over 5000 feet.
    🔗
    Though not present on this map, a local minimum or valley would look similar but with decreasing values as you move to the center.
    🔗
    A feature not possible on the two-dimensional plots you studied in Math 151 is a saddle point. This is part of the graph shaped liked a saddle and the point is the place where you would sit on the saddle. On this map, if you draw a line from the peaks at Mauna Loa and Mauna Kea, and then travel north along this line, you would descend from Mauna Loa and then ascend to Mauna Kea where the approximate midpoint would be a minimum value between the peaks, But also at this point you would be at an elevation of about 7000 feet and if the traveled from this point east or west, you would descend again. So at this saddle point. If you look north or south you are looking uphill to one of the peaks, but if you look east or west, you look downhill. So this is mixture of minimum point in one direction and a maximum in other directions.
    🔗
    We will study maximum, minimum and saddle points further in a future section as an application of derivatives.
    🔗
    Two other things that contour maps tell up about a derivative are the rate of change and the directional derivative. If we start one again at the top of the peak of Mauna Loa and draw a line pointing to the right (east). If our line is 40 kilometers long, using the scale along the bottom, this line would extend from the peak at Mauna Loa to a point near the contour at 3000 feet. Along this line we have lost about 10,000 feet in elevation over 40 kilometers giving an average rate of change of -10,000/40 = -250 feet per kilometer. If we travel along a 40-kilometer line south instead we descend to an elevation of 1000 feet, giving a rate of change of -12,000/40 = -300 feet per kilometer. The line south has more contour lines closer together giving a steeper descent than the line east. How steep the descent or accent is measured be the derivative (instantaneous rate of change). This can be different in different directions. It is natural to look at the derivative in the \(x\) and \(y-\)directions, we can form a derivative in any direction. We will see how to do this in a future section.

    Subsection 14.3 Limits and Continuity of Three-Dimensional Functions

    Before we look at more about derivatives, we will close this section looking at limits and continuity (and domain) for multi-valued functions. Just like for single-variable functions in Math 151, the multi-variable functions we will grow to know and love (exponential, logarithm, trigonometric, square root, etc.) are continuous on their domains. Here are three examples of finding the domain of a multi-variable function.
    🔗

    Example 14.8.

    Find the domain of the function: \(f(x,y)=4\ln(y^2-x)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Since the function inside a logarithm must be positive for the logarithm to be defined, we must have
    \begin{equation*} y^2-x \gt 0 \to y^2 \gt x \to x \lt y^2 \end{equation*}
    This defines a region in the \(xy-\)plane shaped like a parabola with a vertex at the origin and opening to the right. The domain is the region inside this parabola.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 14.9.

    Find the domain of the function: \(f(x,y)=4x^2+y^2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Since the function does not include a logarithm, square root or denominator that would restrict the domain, the domain is the entire \(xy-\)plane. Mathematicians shorthand this with \(\mathbb{R}^2\) pronounced "artoo" like everyone’s favorite droid from Star Wars.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 14.10.

    Find the domain of the function: \(f(x,y)=\sqrt{x^2+y^2-4}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Since the function inside a square root must be non-negative, we must have
    \begin{equation*} x^2+y^2-4 \geq 0 \to x^2+y^2 \geq 4 \end{equation*}
    Since \(x^2+y^2 = 4\) is a circle in the \(xy-\)plane, the domain is the region on or outside the circle.
    🔗
    🔗
    Knowing the domain of our function is the same as knowing where a function is continuous for the functions that we will be studying. It can be more complicated for some special types of functions like piece-wise defined functions or other strange functions that mathematicians have dreamed up. We can formally define continuity using the usual calculus definition that a function is continuos at a point \((a,b)\) if
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{(x,y)\to (a,b)} f(x,y) = f(a,b) \end{equation*}
    Note that when we calculate limits for multi-variable functions, we take a limit for both \(x\) and \(y\) at the same time. This leads to a new possibility to consider for limits that we will see in the final example below.
    🔗

    Example 14.11.

    Calculate the limit
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{(x,y)\to(1,2)} \frac{3x+y}{x^2+y^2+1} \end{equation*}
    Since this function has no restrictions on the domain since the denominator is always positive, we can calculate the limit by direct substitution.
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{(x,y)\to(1,2)} \frac{3x+y}{x^2+y^2+1} = \frac{3(1)+2}{1^2+2^2+1} = \frac{5}{6} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 14.12.

    Calculate the limit
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{(x,y)\to(3,0)} \frac{x^3\sin(y)}{y} \end{equation*}
    For this function \(y\ne 0\text{.}\) But we can rewrite this function as a product of \(x\) and \(y\) functions
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{(x,y)\to(3,0)} \frac{x^3\sin(y)}{y} = \left(\lim_{x \to 3} x^3\right)\left(\lim_{y \to 0} \frac{\sin(y)}{y}\right) = (27)(1) = 27 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 14.13.

    Calculate the limit
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)} \frac{x^2}{x^2+y^2} \end{equation*}
    This function is only undefined at the origin, which is also our limiting point. If we approach this limit from different directions, we will get different results.
    \begin{equation*} \text{along the x-axis}\,\,(y=0) \quad \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{x^2}{x^2+0^2} = 1 \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \text{along the y-axis}\,\,(0=0) \quad \lim_{y \to 0} \frac{0^2}{0^2+y^2} = 0 \end{equation*}
    Along the different paths to the origin, we get different results. This is analogous to the right and left-hand limits not being equal in Calculus I. If we get different results from different "sides" then the limit does not exist! Try graphing this to explore what is happening at the origin for this function.
