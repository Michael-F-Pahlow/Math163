    Section 17 Lagrange Multipliers

    The method of Lagrange multipliers is a way to solve an optimization problem for a multi-variable function subject to one or more constraint equations. The method is named after the French mathematician Joseph-Louis Lagrange who formulated the method in the 18th century. Lagrange also formulated the mean value theorem and succeeded Euler at the Berlin Academy at the invitation of Frederick the Great. His name is also one of the 72 great French men of science on the Eiffel tower. (The names of 72 great French women of science are currently being added.)

    Subsection 17.1 A Lagrange Multipliers by Hand

    The method of Lagrange multipliers adds an extra variable, the Lagrange multiplier and usually denoted with \(\lambda\) for each constraint equation, setting up a system of equations where we solve for the value of the variables and the Lagrange multiplier at the maximum or minimum for a given function. So if we have a function, \(f(x,y,z)\) that we want to optimize that is subject to a constraint equation \(g(x,y,z)=C\text{,}\) then the Lagrange multiplier is used in the equation \(\nabla f(x,y,z) = \lambda \nabla g(x,y,z) \text{.}\) This generates three equations and four unknowns. Adding the constraint equation gives a fourth equation, so overall, we would have system with four equations and four unknowns to solve.
    🔗

    Example 17.1.

    Find the extreme values of the function \(f(x,y) = x^2 + 2y^2\) on the circle \(x^2+y^2 = 1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    \(\nabla f(x,y) = \lambda \nabla g(x,y)\) plus the constraint equation gives the three equations:
    \begin{align*} 2x =\amp\lambda (2x) \\ 4y = \amp \lambda (2y) \\ x^2+y^2 \amp = 1 \end{align*}
    🔗
    From the first equation we have \(\lambda = 1\) or \(x=0\text{.}\) If \(x=0\text{,}\) then the last equation gives \(y= \pm 1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    If \(\lambda = 1\text{,}\) then the second equation gives \(y=0\text{.}\) Plugging this in the last equation gives \(x=\pm 1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    So we have four solution points: (0, 1), (0, -1), (1, 0) and (-1, 0). Evaluating at these four points gives maximum values for \(f(x,y)\) of 2 at (0, 1) and (0,-1) and minimum values of 1 at (1, 0) and (-1, 0).
    🔗
    This process of Lagrange multipliers is straightforward but can be laborious. Fortunately, there is a way for Excel to do the work for us!
