    Section 19 Double Integrals Over General Regions

    We need not use rectangles in the \(xy-\)plane as the base for our volume when calculating double integrals. We can integrate over any region in the \(xy-\)plane.
    🔗

    Objectives: Double Integrals Over General Regions

    In this section we will look at:
    🔗
    • Calculating double integrals over various-shaped regions
      🔗
      🔗
    • Switching the order of integration.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 19.1 Double Integrals in General

    For more general regions in the \(xy-\)plane we have either
    \begin{equation*} \int_a^b \int_{y=f(x)}^{y=g(x)}f(x,y) dy dx \quad \text{or}\quad \int_c^d \int_{x=f(y)}^{x=g(y)}f(x,y) dx dy \end{equation*}
    We must always have constants as limits on the outer integral to evaluate the integral to a numerical result in the end.
    🔗
    Like the area between curves in Calculus II (Math 152) it is helpful to sketch (or use technology) a graph of the region we are integrating over to help setup the integral.
    🔗

    Example 19.1.

    Find the volume under the surface \(f(x,y)=x^2y\) over the region bounded by \(y=\sqrt{x}\text{,}\) \(y=1/x\) and \(x=3\text{.}\) A graph of this region looks like
    🔗
    Since \(y=\sqrt{x}\) always defines the top of this region and \(y=1/x\) always defines the bottom of the region and \(x\) is bounded by constants 1 and 3, we will integrate with respect to \(y\) first.
    \begin{equation*} \int_1^3 \int_{y=1/x}^{y=\sqrt{x}} x^2y dy dx = \int_1^3 \left(\left. \frac{1}{2}x^2y^2 \right\vert_{y=1/x}^{y=\sqrt{x}}\right) dx = \int_1^3 \left(\frac{x^3}{2}- \frac{1}{2}\right)dx \end{equation*}
    Now integrate with respect to \(x\text{.}\)
    \begin{equation*} \int_1^3 \left(\frac{x^3}{2}- \frac{1}{2}\right)dx = \left.\left(\frac{x^4}{8}-\frac{x}{2}\right \vert_1^3 \right) = 9 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 19.3.

    Calculate the volume under the surface \(f(x,y)=2x+3y\) over the region bounded by \(y=x\text{,}\) \(y=x/2\) and \(y=2\text{.}\) A graph of the region looks like
    🔗
    Here \(x=2y\) is always on the right and \(x=y\) is always on the left. \(y\) is bounded by the constants 0 and 2, so we integrate with respect to \(x\) first.
    \begin{equation*} \int_0^2 \int_{x=y}^{x=2y} (2x+3y)dx dy = \int_0^2 \left(\left. (x^2 + 3xy)\right\vert_{x=y}^{x=2y}\right) dy = \int_0^2 6y^2dy \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \int_0^2 6y^2dy = \left. 2y^3\right\vert_0^2 = 16 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 19.5.

    Calculate the volume under the surface \(f(x,y)=e^{y^2}\) over the region bounded by \(y=2\text{,}\) \(y=x/2\) and \(x=0\text{.}\) A graph of the region looks like
    🔗
    Here would could setup the double integral as either
    \begin{equation*} \int_0^4 \int_{y=x/2}^2 e^{y^2} dy dx \,\,\,\text{or} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \int_0^2 \int_{x=0}^{2y} e^{y^2} dx dy \end{equation*}
    The first integral is impossible to calculate by hand since \(e^{y^2}\) has no explicit antiderivative, so we will calculate the second version
    \begin{equation*} \int_0^2 \int_{x=0}^{2y} e^{y^2} dx dy = \int_0^2 \left(\left. xe^{y^2}\right\vert_0^{2y}\right)dx = \int_0^2 2ye^{y^2} dy \end{equation*}
    Now we can use a substitution to calculate this integral. Let \(s=y^2\) then
    \begin{equation*} \int_0^2 2ye^{y^2} dy = \int_0^4 e^s ds = e^4 - 1 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    For this last example we saw how that we can setup the double integral in either order of variables. It is theoretically possible to always do this, but sometimes one order is easier based on the function we are integrating. And sometimes it is easier to setup the limits of integration for one order of integration than the other order of integration. We will best look at how to switch the order of integration when the limits are not constants. Here is a video with another example

    Subsection 19.2 Switching the Order of Integration

    It is easiest to switch the order of integration by sketching the region given by a set of limits of integration and then write a new integral with the other order of variables.
    🔗

    Example 19.8.

    Evaluate the integral by changing the order of integration.
    \begin{equation*} \int_1^9\int_{\sqrt{y}}^3 xe^y dx dy \end{equation*}
    A graph of the region based on the limits of integration looks like this. (See the left input panel for how I entered this in Desmos.)
    🔗
    From the figure we can see that we can rewrite the double integral as
    \begin{equation*} \int_1^9\int_{\sqrt{y}}^3 xe^y dx dy = \int_1^3 \int_1^{y=x^2} xe^y dy dx = \int_1^3 \left(\left. xe^y\right\vert_1^{x^2}\right)dx = \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \int_1^3(xe^{x^2} - xe) dx = \left.\frac{1}{2}(e^{x^2}-x^2e)\right\vert_1^3 = \frac{1}{2}(e^9-9e) \end{equation*}
