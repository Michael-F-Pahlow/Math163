    Section 9 The Cross Product

    We have seen two ways to multiply with a vector. Scalar multiplication multiplies a scalar times a vectors and the result is a rescaled vector. The Dot product multiplies two vectors by multiplying the like components from each vector together and then adding the results which gives a scalar. A third way to multiply vectors is called the cross product. The cross product is only defined for three-dimensional vectors unlike the other two types.
    🔗

    Objectives: The Cross Product

    In this section we will look at:
    🔗
    • The definition of the cross product
      🔗
      🔗
    • Properties of the cross product
      🔗
      🔗
    • Applications of the cross product
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 9.1 Definition of the Cross Product

    The cross product is defined as the determinant of a \(3\times 3\) matrix consisting of the two vectors and the \(\mathbf{\hat{i}}\text{,}\) \(\mathbf{\hat{j}}\) and \(\mathbf{\hat{k}}\) unit vectors for the three dimensions.
    🔗

    The Cross Product.

    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{v} = \left|\begin{array}{ccc} \hat{i} \amp \hat{j} \amp \hat {k} \\ u_1 \amp u_2 \amp u_3 \\ v_1 \amp v_2 \amp v_3 \\ \end{array} \right| \end{equation*}
    Which is calculated as follows
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{v} = (u_2 v_3 - u_3 v_2)\mathbf{\hat{i}} - (u_1 v_3 - u_3 v_3)\mathbf{\hat{j}} + (u_1 v_2 - u_2 v_1)\mathbf{\hat{k}} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    This may look daunting, but there is a quick way to calculate the cross product which with a little practice makes things easy. I learned this in a physics class in college and still do it this way 25 years later.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 9.2 Properties of the Cross Product

    Just like the dot product, the cross product has some important algebraic properties and a couple of nice geometric properties
    🔗

    Algebraic Properties of the Cross Product.

    \begin{align*} \text{Anticommutative} \,\,\,\,\,\,\,\, \amp \mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{v} = - \mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{u}\\ \text{Distributive} \,\,\,\,\,\,\,\, \amp \mathbf{u}\times(\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{w}) = \mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{w}\\ \text{Zero Product} \,\,\,\,\,\,\,\, \amp \mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{0} = \mathbf{0}\times\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}\\ \text{A vector cross itself} \,\,\,\,\,\,\,\, \amp \mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}\\ \text{Distribution with a scalar}\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\, \amp c(\mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{c}) = (c\mathbf{u})\times\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{u}\times(c\mathbf{v})\\ \text{Scalar triple product} \,\,\,\,\,\,\,\, \amp \mathbf{u}\cdot(\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{w}) = (\mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{v})\cdot\mathbf{w} \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Note that the cross product is anticommutative so that if you switch the order, you switch the sign. Also note that when you cross a vector with itself, the result is a zero vector. This is due to the geometric properties of the cross product.
    🔗

    Geometric Properties of the Cross Product.

    When you cross two vectors, the resulting vector is orthogonal to both of the vectors you crossed. For the component unit vectors \(\mathbf{\hat{i}}\text{,}\) \(\mathbf{\hat{j}}\) and \(\mathbf{\hat{k}}\) we get the cross products:\(\mathbf{\hat{i}}\times\mathbf{\hat{j}} = \mathbf{\hat{k}}\text{,}\) \(\mathbf{\hat{j}}\times\mathbf{\hat{k}} = \mathbf{\hat{i}}\) and \(\mathbf{\hat{k}}\times\mathbf{\hat{i}} = \mathbf{\hat{j}}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The magnitude of the cross product is defined as
    \begin{equation*} ||\mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{v}|| = ||\mathbf{u}||\cdot||\mathbf{v}||\sin(\theta) \end{equation*}
    This is why a vector crossed with itself is zero, since the angle between the a vector and itself zero and \(\sin(0) = 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 9.3 Applications of the Cross Product

    We will close this section by looking at examples of using the cross product. One geometric and one physics related.
    🔗

    Example 9.2. Area of a Parallelogram.

    The area of a parallelogram that is defined be vectors for two adjoining sides is equal to the magnitude of the cross product.
    🔗
    The area of a parallelogram is equal to base times height. As we can see in this figure, the base is the magnitude of \(\mathbf{u}\text{,}\) so the area is \(A = ||\mathbf{u}||\cdot||\mathbf{v}||\sin(\theta) = ||\mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{v}||\text{.}\)
    🔗
    So given a parallelogram formed by the vectors \(\langle -1, 2, 3\rangle\) and \(\langle -2, -1, -4\rangle\) the area is \(\langle -1, 2, 3\rangle\times\langle -2, -1, -4\rangle = \langle -5, -10, 5 \rangle\) and the magnitude of this vector is \(\sqrt{150}.\)
    🔗
    🔗
    The area of triangle where two sides are formed from adjoining vectors is half the area of a parallelogram so \(A = \frac{1}{2}||\mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{v}||\) and the volume of a parallelepiped - the three-dimensional analog of a parallelogram is the magnitude of the scalar triple product. One final example to end this section.
    🔗

    Example 9.4. Torque.

    Several things in physics are defined as a cross product, one of those is torque \(\tau = \mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{F}\) where \(\mathbf{r}\) is the radius of the axis of the object to be rotated and \(\mathbf{F}\) is the force, Torque measures the tendency of a force to produce rotation about an axis.
    🔗
    Suppose 6 Newton force is used to a tighten a bolt using a 15-centimeter wrench starting from an angle of 40 degrees from the horizontal. Find the amount of torque on the bolt.
    🔗
    We want to find the amount of torque which would be the magnitude of torque. We are given the magnitude of the force and the radius (wrench length), so that we can use the magnitude of the cross product formula. We also need to be careful about units. Force is measured in Newtons, so radius needs to be in meters (15 cm = 0.15 m) so that the result is in Newton-meters or Joules.
    \begin{equation*} ||\tau|| = (0.15)(6)\sin(40^{\circ}) \approx 0.578 \,\,\, \text{Joules} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
