    Section 8 The Dot Product

    We saw in the last section one way to with vectors is scalar multiplication. When you multiply a scalar times a vector the result is a rescaled vector. A second way to multiply vectors is the \(dot product\text{.}\) Here we will multiply two vectors in such a way that the result will be a scalar.
    🔗

    Objectives: The Dot Product

    In this section we will look at:
    🔗
    • The definition of the dot product
      🔗
      🔗
    • Properties of the dot product
      🔗
      🔗
    • Applications of the dot product
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 8.1 Definition of the Dot Product

    Given two vectors \(\mathbf{u} = \langle a, b, c \rangle\) and \(\mathbf{v} = \langle x ,y ,z \rangle\text{,}\) then dot product is:
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v} = ax + by + cz. \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Example 8.1.

    Suppose you are a company that produces and sells smartphones, TVs and laptops. Smartphones sell for $400, TVs sell for $1000 and laptops sell for $900. Further suppose that you sell 200 smartphones, 300 TVs and 500 laptops, how much total revenue would you generate?
    🔗
    We can create two vectors, one with the revenue per unit for each it and another vector for each quantity sold:
    \begin{align*} \mathbf{R} = \amp \langle \text{\$}400, \text{\$}1000, \text{\$}900 \rangle \,\,\amp \text{revenue in dollars per item}\\ \mathbf{Q} = \amp \langle 200, 300, 500 \rangle\,\, \amp\text{quantity in number of untis sold} \end{align*}
    Multiplying revenue per item times number of units sold will give the total dollar amount generated from the sale of each item. If we add up the total dollar amount for each item, we will get the overall total revenue. But this is the exact definition of the dot product!
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{R}\cdot\mathbf{Q} = 400\cdot 200 + 1000\cdot 300 + 900\cdot 500 = \text{\$}830,000 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 8.2 Properties of the Dot Product

    The dot product has several algebraic properties and one important geometric property. The algebraic properties are what you would expect from an operation defined by multiplying and adding:
    🔗

    Properties of the Dot Product.

    \begin{align*} \text{Commutative}\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\, \amp \mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{u}\\ \text{Distributive} \,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\amp \mathbf{u}\cdot(\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{w}) = \mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{w}\\ \text{Associative}\,\,\,\,\, \,\,\,\,\,\amp c(\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}) = (c\mathbf{u})\cdot\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{u}\cdot(c\mathbf{v})\\ \text{Zero Product}\,\,\,\,\, \,\,\,\,\,\amp \mathbf{u}\cdot\langle 0,0,0\rangle = 0\\ \text{Magnitude} \,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\amp \mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{v} = x^2 + y^2 + z^2 ||\mathbf{v}||^2 \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 8.2. The Commutative Property.

    \begin{equation*} \langle -7, 5, 4 \rangle\cdot\langle 0,1,0 \rangle = 5 =\langle 0,1,0\rangle\cdot\langle -7, 5, 4 \rangle \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 8.3. Distributive Property.

    \begin{equation*} \langle 2, 1, -3\rangle(\langle 4, 1, 9\rangle \cdot \langle -5, 0, -6 \rangle) = \langle 2, 1, -3\rangle\cdot\langle -1, 1, 3 \rangle = -10 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \langle 2, 1, -3\rangle\cdot\langle 4, 1, 9\rangle + \langle 2, 1, -3\rangle\cdot\langle -5, 0, -6\rangle = -18 + 8 = -10 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Another important property of the dot product is its geometric interpretation
    🔗

    The Geometry of the Dot Product.

    Given two vectors \(\mathbf{u}\) and \(\mathbf{v}\) with an angle of \(\theta\) between them
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v} = ||\mathbf{u}||\cdot||\mathbf{v}||\cos(\theta) \end{equation*}
    Note that if the vectors are separated by a right angle, then the dot product equals zero since \(\cos(90^{\circ}) = 0.\) If this is the case then the vectors are called orthogonal.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 8.4.

    If a 3-meter long vector and a 2-meter long vector are separated by and angle of 60 degrees, what is the dot product of these two vectors?
    🔗
    Since we are given the magnitudes of the two vectors and the angle between them:
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v} = (3)(2)\cos(60^{\circ}) = (3)(2)(1/2) = 3 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 8.5.

    What is the angle between the vectors \(\langle 2,1\rangle\) and \(\langle -1,3 \rangle\text{?}\)
    🔗
    First, we know that \(\langle 2,1\rangle\cdot\langle -1,3\rangle = 1\) and second, we can calculate the magnitude of each vector.
    \begin{equation*} ||\langle 2,1\rangle|| = \sqrt{5} \,\,\, \text{and} \,\,\, ||\langle -1, 3\rangle|| = \sqrt{10} \end{equation*}
    Plugging this all into the geometric definition for the dot product gives:
    \begin{equation*} 1 = \sqrt{5}\sqrt{10}\cos(\theta) \end{equation*}
    and solving for \(\theta\) gives:
    \begin{equation*} \theta = \arccos\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{50}}\right) \approx 1.429 \,\,\, \text{radians} \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Subsection 8.3 Applications of the Dot Product

    We will finish this section with a few examples of applications of the dot product.
    🔗

    Example 8.7. Velocity.

    A container ship leaves port traveling on a course of 15 degrees north of east. Its engine generates a speed of 20 knots along that path. THe ocean current moves the ship northeast at a speed of 2 knots. Considering both the engine and the current, how fast is the ship traveling along its course?
    🔗
    We know that the ship’s engines contribute a speed of 20 knots along its course. We want to know how much the ocean current contributes to this speed. We are given speed (magnitude of velocity) and direction for both the ship and the current from which we can form the vectors:
    \begin{align*} \text{Ship}\,\,\,\, \amp\mathbf{S} =\langle 20\cos(15^{\circ}),20\sin(15^{\circ})\rangle \approx \langle 19.319, 5.176 \rangle \\ \text{Current}\,\,\,\,\amp \mathbf{C} =\langle 2\cos(45^{\circ}),2\sin(45^{\circ})\rangle \approx \langle 1.414, 1.414 \rangle \end{align*}
    Here the components point in the \(x\) and \(y\)-directions. We want a vector for the current that points in the direction of the ship’s course. This is called the scalar projection and is denoted by
    \begin{equation*} \text{comp}_\mathbf{S} \mathbf{C} = \frac{\mathbf{S}\cdot\mathbf{C}}{||\mathbf{S}} = \frac{||\mathbf{S}||\cdot||\mathbf{C}||\cos{\theta}}{||\mathbf{S}||}=||\mathbf{C}||\cos(45^{\circ}-15^{\circ}) = 2\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} \end{equation*}
    Which is approximately 1.73 knots. Adding this to the 20 knots generated by the ship’s engines and the ship is traveling at 21.73 knots along its course.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 8.8. Work.

    Suppose a wagon is pulled 100 meters along a horizontal path by a constant of force of 70 Newtons. THe handle of the wagon is held at a angle of 35 degrees above the horizontal. How much work is done by the force?
    🔗
    Work is defined as a force vector dotted with a displacement vector. In this example, we are given the magnitudes of a force vector (70 Newtons) and a displacement vector (100 meters) as well as the angle between these vectors. So:
    \begin{equation*} \text{Work} = W = \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{D} = (70)(100)\cos(35^{\circ}) \approx 5734.06\,\,\,\text{Joules} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 8.9. Bond Angle.

    A molecule of methane, CH_4, is structured with the four hydrogen atoms at the four vertices of a regular tetrahedron and the carbon at the centroid of the tetrahedron. The bond angle is the angle formed by the H-C-H combination. What is the bond angle?
    🔗
    Since we are only after the angle we can model our molecule as a tetrahedron with vertices at the points (1, 0, 0), (0, 1, 0), (0, 0, 1) and (1, 1, 1). This puts the centroid at \((\frac{1}{2}, \frac{1}{2}, \frac{1}{2})\text{.}\) We can then form one vector extending from the centroid to (1, 0, 0) and get \(\langle \frac{1}{2}, -\frac{-}{2}, -\frac{1}{2}\text{.}\) Another vector from the centroid to (0, 1, 0) is \(\langle -\frac{1}{2}, \frac{1}{2}, -\frac{1}{2} \rangle\text{.}\) The dot product of these vectors is \(-\frac{1}{4}\text{.}\) You can check to see that this would be the dot product no matter which two vertices we choose. The magnitude of each of these vectors is \(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\text{.}\)The bond angle is then:
    \begin{equation*} -\frac{1}{4} = \left(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right)\left(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right)\cos(\theta) \rightarrow \theta = \arccos\left(-\frac{1}{3}\right) \approx 1.9106 \approx 109.47^{\circ} \end{equation*}
    🔗
