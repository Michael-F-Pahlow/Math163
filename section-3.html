    Section 3 Power Series

    A power series is an infinite polynomial that all of the functions we know and love are equal to.
    \begin{equation*} f(x) = a_0 + a_1 x+ a_2 x^2 + a_3 x^3 + ... = \sum_{n=0}^\infty a_nx^n \end{equation*}
    Power series are a foundational part of the study of functions. I have a calculus textbook from the 1950’s that covers power series first before it covers limits, derivatives and integrals. (Calculus by Tomlinson Fort, D. C. Heath and Company, Boston, 1951). Power series are also how a calculator or computer actually calculates function values. When you type <sin(5)> into your calculator, it is not actually actually evaluating the sine function but instead using the related power series for sine to make its calculation! For now, I will introduce a few power series for well-known functions and when they converge and diverge before we look at how we can build a power series from scratch in the next section.

    Subsection 3.1 Some Well Known Power Series

    We will now look at a few examples of some well-known power series and also a couple of examples of how we can use a known power series to find a new power series.
    🔗

    Example 3.1. A Geometric Series.

    One power series we can define easily is a series base on a geometric series. We saw in the last section that
    \begin{equation*} \sum_{n=0}^\infty ar^n = \frac{a}{1-r} \end{equation*}
    If we let \(a=1\) and \(r=x\) then
    \begin{equation*} f(x) = \sum_{n=0}^\infty x^n = \frac{1}{1-x} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 3.2. Another Geometric Series.

    We can find other similar series by some factoring and substitution.
    \begin{equation*} f(x) = \frac{1}{3-6x} = \frac{1}{3}\cdot\frac{1}{1-(2x)} = \frac{1}{3}\sum_{n=0}^\infty (2x)^n = \sum_{n=0} \frac{2^n}{3} x^n \end{equation*}
    We try to write a power series as a product of sequence of coefficients times powers of \(x\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 3.3. A Third Geometric Series.

    \begin{equation*} f(x) = \frac{1}{1+x^2} = \frac{1}{1-(-x^2)} = \sum_{n=0}^\infty (-x^2)^n = \sum_{n=0}^\infty (-1)^n x^{2n} = 1 - x^2 + x^4 - x^6 +... \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 3.4. Power Series of \(\arctan(x)\).

    Since a power series is just an infinite polynomial, we can differentiate or integrate a known power series using the power rule to get a new power series. Since
    \begin{equation*} \int \frac{1}{1+x^2} dx = \arctan(x) \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \int \sum_{n=0}^\infty (-1)^n x^{2n} = \sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{(-1)^n x^{2n+1}}{2n+1} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 3.5. A Power Series from Differentiation.

    Since:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{d}{dx} \left(\frac{1}{1-x}\right) = \frac{1}{(1-x)^2} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \frac{d}{dx} \sum_{n=0}^\infty x^n = \sum_{n=1}^\infty (n)x^{n-1} \end{equation*}
    Note that when differentiating, we change the starting point of our sum from zero to one, since the \(a_0\) is a constant in the starting series and becomes zero when we take the derivative and the new series starts at the \(a_1\) term.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 3.6. Power Series of Sine, Cosine and \(e^x\).

    Three of the most used series are the series for \(\sin(x), \, \cos(x)\) and \(e^x\text{.}\) We will see in the next section, how to build these series from scratch. for now we define.
    \begin{align*} e^x = \amp \sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{x^n}{n!}\\ \sin(x) = \amp \sum_{n=0}^\infty (-1)^n\frac{x^{2n+1}}{(2n+1)!}\\ \cos(x)= \amp \sum_{n=0}^\infty (-1)^n\frac{x^{2n}}{(2n)!} \end{align*}
    Note that the derivative of the sine series is the cosine series. Also note if you replace \(x \) in the exponential series with \(ix\) where \(i\) is the imaginary number then the sum of the cosine series plus \(i \times\)the sine series equals the exponential series! (Euler’s formula)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 3.2 Intervals of Convergence

    A key thing we want to know for a power series is where it converges. This is analogous to finding the domain of a function. This is where we need to know a few convergence/divergence tests from the previous section. When we find the \(x-\)values where a series converges, this interval is known as the interval of convergence. For example, the base geometric series is known to converge when \(\vert x\vert \lt 1 \) which can also be written as an interval \((-1, 1)\) or a trilinear inequality \(-1 \lt x \lt 1\)
    🔗

    Example 3.8. An Interval of Convergence that Includes Only One Endpoint.

    Consider the power series:
    \begin{equation*} \sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{(x-3)^n}{n} \end{equation*}
    We need to think about what happens when we plug in various values of \(x\text{.}\) If \(x=4\text{,}\) the resulting series is the harmonic series \(\sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{1}{n}\) which is known to converge. If \(x=2\) we get an alternating series \(\sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{(-1)^n}{n}\) which converges by the alternating series test. If \(2 \lt x \lt 4 \) The result is a geometric-like series which we can use the limit comparison test with a geometric series to show converges. Finally, if the \(x \lt 2\) or \(x \gt 4\text{,}\) then the resulting sequence inside the series would not tend to zero, so it would diverge by the divergence test. Putting this all together gives and interval of convergence of \(2 \leq x \lt 4\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 3.9. An Interval of Convergence that Includes Both Endpoints.

    Consider the power series:
    \begin{equation*} \sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{(x-2)^n}{n^2+1} \end{equation*}
    Again we can try various values of \(x\text{.}\) If \(x=3\text{,}\) the resulting series is one where the sequence inside the series is \(\frac{1}{n^2+1}\) and the series can be shown to converge by the integral test, since \(\int \frac{1}{x^2+1} dx = \arctan(x) \text{.}\) If \(x=1\text{,}\) the resulting series is an alternating series that converges by the alternating series test. Since the series converges at \(x=1\) and \(x=3\text{,}\) it makes sense that it also converges for values between these two points. Like in the previous example, if \(x\) is not between 1 and 3, then the resulting series would diverge by the divergence test.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 3.10. An Infinite Interval of Convergence.

    Consider the series:
    \begin{equation*} \sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{x^n}{(2n-1)!} \end{equation*}
    For any value of \(x\) that we choose, we get something that looks like a geometric series over a factorial. If we use the limit comparison test with \(a_n = x^n/(2n-1)!\) and \(b_n = x^n/(2n)!\text{,}\) then the limit would always tend to one. So this power series converges for all values of \(x\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 3.11. An Interval of Convergence that Includes Only One Point.

    Consider the series:
    \begin{equation*} \sum_{n=1}^\infty nx^n \end{equation*}
    If we let \(x=0\) then the resulting series would just be \(0+0+0+0+...=0\text{.}\) For any other value of \(x\text{,}\) the resulting series would diverge by the divergence test. So this series only converges for \(x=0\text{.}\)
