    Section 6 2D Vectors

    A vector is a directed line with a starting point, ending point and an arrow pointing in a specified direction. Vectors have many uses in physics and engineering such as the calculation of work and torque. Since vectors are by definition geometric objects, they are also useful in mathematics for calculating things like area and volume. Vectors are also widely used in Linear Algebra (Math 238) and Calculus IV (Math 254).
    🔗

    Subsection 6.1 Definitions and Notation

    While a vector can start and end at any point, it is convenient to use the origin (0, 0) as a common starting point. Then the vector can be defined by the ending point. For example if we want a vector that starts at (0,0) and ends at (-5, 2), then we could write the vector as \(\langle -5, 2 \rangle\text{.}\)
    🔗
    There are several ways to write and refer to vectors. When typesetting vectors, like in these notes, it is common to refer to vectors using boldface letter, such as \(\mathbf{v}\text{.}\) When handwriting vectors, it is common to denote vectors with a small arrow written over the letter used for the vector, \(\vec{v}\text{.}\) The vector itself is usually written like a point enclosed in angle brackets, \(\langle, \rangle\text{.}\) The numbers or variables written inside the brackets are called the components.
    🔗
    An alternative way to write a vector, that is common in physics and engineering is to write a vector as a sum of its components where each component is followed by \(\mathbf{\hat{i}}\) or \(\mathbf{\hat{j}}\) pronounced as i-hat and j-hat. \(\mathbf{\hat{i}}\) corresponds to the \(x-\)component and \(\mathbf{\hat{j}}\) corresponds to the \(y-\)component. So
    \begin{equation*} \langle -2, 5 \rangle = -2\mathbf{\hat{i}} + 5\mathbf{\hat{j}} \end{equation*}
    This is called i-j-k notation. (We’ll add the \(\mathbf{\hat{k}}\) in the next section.)
    🔗
    When studying vectors, we want to distinguish them from regular numbers (or variables) which we call scalars. Scalars are written as normal and typeset in regular text. One reason we want to distinguish between scalars and vectors is that while there is only way to multiply scalars, there are three different ways to multiply with vectors!
    🔗
    Two other important properties of vectors are their magnitude and direction. The magnitude of a vector is its length. We use \(||\mathbf{v}||\) to denote the magnitude of a vector. To find the length of slanted line which forms the hypotenuse of a right triangle, we use the Pythagorean theorem. So
    \begin{equation*} ||\mathbf{v}|| = \sqrt{x^2+y^2} \end{equation*}
    where \(x\) and \(y\) are the components of the vector.
    🔗
    The direction of a two-dimensional vector is the angle, in radians, it makes with the positive \(x\)-axis. We define this as \(\theta = \arctan(y/x) \text{.}\) The arctangent only gives positive angles in the first and quadrant, so if our vector is pointing in the second or third quadrant, we need to add \(\pi\) to our result and to get a positive result in the fourth quadrant, we need to add \(2\pi\text{.}\)
    🔗
    If we know the magnitude and direction of a two-dimensional vector, we can write the components of the vector using the formulas. \(x = ||\mathbf{v}||\cos(\theta) \) and \(y =||\mathbf{v}||\sin(\theta)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    If this all looks familiar, converting from component form to direction and magnitude is the same thing as converting from rectangular coordinates to polar coordinates.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 6.2 Vector Operations and Properties

    Similar to scalars, vectors can be added and multiplied. Subtraction is just adding negative numbers and division is a little more complicated because of the three ways that vectors can be used in multiplication. Vector addition/subtraction is done component-wise.
    🔗

    Example 6.3. Vector Addition.

    \begin{equation*} \langle 5, 4 \rangle + \langle -3, 7 \rangle = \langle 5-3, 4+7 \rangle = \langle 2, 11 \rangle \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Vector addition/subtraction, like many aspects of vectors that we shall see, has a geometric interpretation. Adding or subtracting two vectors gives the diagonal of a parallelogram. The longer diagonal represents addition and the shorter diagonal represents subtraction. We can see this in the following Desmos demonstration. Click on Vector u + v or Vector u -v and on the Sides of parallelogram
    🔗
    You can also change the components of the vector \(\mathbf{u}\) by using the sliders for a and b. You can change the components of the vector \(\mathbf{v}\) by using the sliders for c and d. As you move change the vectors, the sum always stays as the longer diagonal of the parallelogram and the difference is the shorter diagonal.
    🔗
    This "parallelogram" law shows two more things about vector addition. One is that vector addition is commutative. If you change the order of addition, you get the same result. Starting at the origin, \(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}\) would represent the two sides of the parallelogram below the diagonal, when all of the components are positive and \(\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{u}\) would represent the two sides of the parallelogram above the diagonal. A second thing we see is that subtraction is anti-commutative.
    \begin{equation*} ||\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v}|| = ||\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{u}|| \end{equation*}
    (same magnitude = same length) but the vectors point in the opposite direction.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 6.3 Applications of Vectors

    We will close this section be looking at a few examples of how we can apply two-dimensional vectors.
    🔗

    Example 6.5. Forces.

    Three forces act on object: \(\mathbf{F}_1 = \langle 2, 5 \rangle, \mathbf{F}_2= \langle 8, 3 \rangle, \mathbf{F}_3 = \langle 0, -7 \rangle\text{.}\) Find the net force on the object.
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{F}_{net} = \mathbf{F}_1 + \mathbf{F}_2 + \mathbf{F}_3 = \langle 10, -2\rangle \end{equation*}
    Note that an equal force pointing in the opposite direction (Newton’s third law) would "balance" the net force. This is the vector \(\langle -10, 2\rangle\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 6.6. A Long Walk.

    A person starts walking from home and walks 4 miles east, 7 miles southeast, 6 miles south, 5 miles southwest and 3 miles east. How far have they walked?
    🔗
    Note that the walk information is given as magnitudes and directions. The total distance walked is the sum of the magnitudes - 24 miles. A very long walk, this is more than my maximum daily hike total of 22 miles.
    🔗
    If they walked straight home, how far would they have to walk and and in what direction?
    🔗
    As we can see in the figure above, where each vector has been converted to component form from the given magnitudes and directions, the distance home is the magnitude of the vector that stretch from home to the tip of the arrow of \(\mathbf{w}_5\text{.}\) This is also the sum of the five walk vectors.
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{w}_{total} = \mathbf{w}_1 +\mathbf{w}_2 +\mathbf{w}_3 +\mathbf{w}_4 + \mathbf{w}_5 = \langle 1+\sqrt{2}, -6-6\sqrt{2} \rangle \approx \langle 2.41, -14.49 \rangle \end{equation*}
    The magnitude of this vector is approximately 14.68 miles. To find the direction, home lets add the total walk vector to the figure.
    🔗
    The direction of the total velocity vector is \(\theta = \arctan(-14.48/2.41) \approx -1.406 \) radians. This angle is in the fourth quadrant, but the direction home is clearly pointing in the second quadrant. This is because the total walk vector points from home to the tip of the fifth walk vector. To get the direction home, we can simply reverse the direction of this vector by rotating it 180 degrees or \(\pi\) radians. So the direction home is \(\theta \approx -1.406 + \pi \approx 1.736\) radians.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 6.9. Velocity.

    Suppose an airplane is heading north at an airspeed of 500 kilometers per hour, but there is a wind blowing from the southeast at 80 kilometers per hour. How many degrees off course will the plane end up flying and what is the speeds relative to the ground?
    🔗
    Velocity can be considered as a vector in physics. The information for the plane and wind’s velocity are given as magnitudes and directions. Converting these to component form gives with the direction north at 90 degrees and the wind direction blowing from the southeast to the northwest at 135 degrees
    \begin{align*} \text{Plane:}\,\, \mathbf{P} = \amp \langle 500\cos(90^{\circ}), 500\sin(90^{\circ}) = \langle 0, 500 \rangle\\ \text{Wind:}\,\, \mathbf{W} = \amp \langle 80\cos(135^{\circ}), 80\sin(135^{\circ}) = \langle -40\sqrt{2}, 40\sqrt{2}\rangle \end{align*}
    Adding these velocity vectors gives the total velocity vector \(\langle -40\sqrt{2}, 500+40\sqrt{2} \rangle \approx \langle -56.57, 556.57\rangle\text{.}\) Find the magnitude of this vector will give the plane’s relative speed. The magnitude is speed = \(\sqrt{(-56.57)^2+5556.57^2}\approx 559.436\) kilometers per hour.
    🔗
    Finding the direction of the total velocity vector and subtracting it from 90 degrees (the plane’s intended direction) will give that angle that the plane is off course. Direction = \(\arctan(-556.57/56.57) + \pi\approx 0.101\) radians. Converting this to degrees gives 5.8 degrees off course.
